1. Direct Sequence Spread Spectrum Principle
The principle of direct sequence spread spectrum (DSSS) is to expand the spectrum of the original signal into a wider frequency band for transmission, while the demodulation principle employs correlation reception to restore the expanded spectrum back to the original signal's own spectrum. In Shannon information theory, it is defined as a Gaussian white noise channel with a bandwidth B and a signal-to-noise ratio S/N, where the channel capacity is:
From the above formula, it is known that the channel capacity C is related to the bandwidth B and the signal-to-noise ratio S/N. To maintain constant channel capacity C, when the bandwidth B decreases, the signal-to-noise ratio S/N must increase. Conversely, if the signal-to-noise ratio S/N decreases, correspondingly increasing the bandwidth B is necessary to keep the channel capacity C constant. Therefore, under the premise of constant channel capacity C, by sacrificing bandwidth B, one can achieve higher interference resistance. Spread spectrum communication can expand the frequency spectrum of signals by up to 10 times or even 100 times, 1000 times, thus obtaining significantly greater bandwidth than conventional communication methods. This allows for enhanced interference resistance, ensuring reliable information transmission in strong interference conditions.

2. BPSK Spread Spectrum Modulation in AWGN Channels
2.1 Principle of BPSK Spread Spectrum Modulation and Demodulation
1. Baseband signals often contain a significant amount of DC and low-frequency components, which are not suitable for direct transmission over wireless channels. They require frequency spectrum shifting, hence the baseband signal needs to be modulated onto a carrier to shift its spectrum to a specific band suitable for wireless channel transmission. During the binary digital modulation process, when the phase of the sinusoidal carrier changes discretely with the binary digital baseband signal, a binary BPSK (Binary Phase Shift Keying) signal is generated. The time domain expression of the BPSK signal is:
When sending the binary symbol "0" code (an = +1), sBPSK(t) takes a phase of 0; when sending the binary symbol "1" code (an = 1), sBPSK(t) takes a phase. BPSK signals are usually demodulated using coherent detection, where the carrier is in-phase and at the same frequency as the received BPSK signal.
